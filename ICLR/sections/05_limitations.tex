\section{Limitations and Future Directions}
\label{sec:limitations}

Six constraints bound these results. First, the design is single-turn: we do not evaluate
multi-turn agentic loops, where a verifier's judgment feeds back into generation and the
generator gets to answer the critique. Second, grounding is a property of our design rather
than of the domains. Mathematics has a checkable final answer that we deliberately withheld
from the judge, so our contrast is between a clean environment signal and none at all, and
we cannot say how verifiers behave under a partial, noisy, or adversarial one. Third,
correctness in the code domain is defined by the same execution result the judge is shown,
so code accuracy partly measures whether a judge reads that signal off correctly rather
than independent code comprehension; the code prompt also explicitly permits overriding
execution, which inflates override rates relative to a neutral instruction. Fourth, the
override audit is LLM-mediated at both ends: one model proposes the adversarial inputs and
a second adjudicates divergences, neither independently validated, over a bounded input
budget. \texttt{NO\_DISCREPANCY} therefore means no divergence was found rather than that
none exists, 14.7\% of overrides never resolved at all, and because these verdicts relabel
ground truth, that uncertainty propagates into every fuzz-adjusted number we report. Fifth,
the judge pool is four open-weight models served by a single provider, one 12B model against
three 70B-class models and no closed frontier system, so any model-level ranking confounds
capability with scale, with provider serving stack, and with output-format compliance: the
12B model produced 262 of the 277 strict-parse failures, and while the regex fallback
recovered every one of them, a model that formats worse may also reason differently. Sixth,
each cell is a single draw at temperature~0 over 150 items from one benchmark per domain.
Our intervals come from stratifying across items and errors, not from decoding variance, and
we cannot rule out benchmark contamination in models whose training corpora are undisclosed.

These point to three directions. Extending grounding to new domains, formal provers for
mathematics and retrieval pipelines for science, would convert our cross-domain contrast
into a within-domain ablation and show how verifiers handle partial or noisy signals rather
than only the two endpoints. Because judges share correlated blind spots precisely where
grounding is absent, training regimes that deliberately decorrelate judges are more
promising than larger panels drawn from the same distribution, which is what our ensemble
results already show. And because self-preference tracks the conclusion a model still holds
rather than the text it wrote, interventions that perturb a verifier's prior are better
targets than authorship blinding: forcing an independent re-derivation before the verdict,
or verifying against a conclusion the judge did not produce.
