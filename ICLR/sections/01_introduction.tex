\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous agent pipelines rely on large language model verifiers to evaluate candidate outputs and actions \citep{zheng2023judging}. In these systems, the verifier's judgment is load-bearing: it gates tool execution and task completion \citep{shinn2024reflexion}, filters synthetic training data \citep{bai2022constitutional}, and terminates iterative self-refinement loops \citep{madaan2023self}. This architecture rests on a critical assumption: that a model's verdict strictly tracks ground-truth correctness rather than superficial attributes of the text. However, existing literature demonstrates that LLM evaluators are systematically biased. They exhibit well-documented sensitivities to response length and candidate ordering \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large}, alongside a pronounced self-preference where models favor their own generated outputs over those of rivals \citep{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024llm}. When a verifier cannot reliably separate factual validity from its own generative habits, the safety and correctness of the entire autonomous pipeline break down.

To mitigate these evaluator failures, practice has converged on two primary fixes. The first treats self-preference as an authorship conflict of interest: prompts are anonymized to blind the judge to author identity \citep{zheng2023judging,dubois2023alpacafarm}, or models are restricted from grading their own outputs \citep{panickssery2024llm}. The second treats unreliability as variance to be averaged away: pipelines deploy stronger frontier judges, majority-vote panels, or multi-agent debate juries \citep{verga2024replacing,chan2024chateval}. However, it remains an open question whether either strategy actually eliminates the source of these failures rather than merely working around them.

To resolve these questions, we construct a large-scale controlled evaluation spanning 64,800 verifications across four models (Qwen2.5-72B, DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, Mistral-Nemo-12B), three domains (HumanEval+ for code, MATH for mathematics, and GPQA-Diamond for science), and three prompting strategies (direct scoring, chain-of-thought, and rubric-guided). In the main evaluation, verifiers assess each candidate individually with a binary verdict under deterministic decoding ($\text{temperature} = 0.0$), reflecting how verifiers operate in real agent execution loops. We cross believed authorship (framed as self-, other-, or neutrally authored) with actual generator identity, employing an error-stratified estimator that holds 779 distinct wrong answers constant across 6,969 strata to isolate belief effects from candidate difficulty and generator quality. While verification in math and science relies strictly on linguistic reasoning, code verification incorporates runtime execution against unit tests, paired with an automated differential fuzzing audit that validates judge overrides against reference implementations using randomized inputs. Finally, to dissect mechanisms and test proposed mitigations, we deploy five targeted probes: a style-transfer probe, a self-recognition probe with consistency control, a structural self-preference probe, a cross-capability oversight and ensemble probe using leave-one-out non-author panels, and a code deliberation jury probe benchmarking parallel debate and sequential role-specialized architectures on the hardest generator.

Across all 64,800 verifications, models achieve an average accuracy of 69.8\%, but performance fractures heavily across domains: 87.7\% in code, 64.5\% in math, and 57.2\% in science. Optimal configurations depend strictly on the domain and architecture: Mistral with direct prompting achieves the highest accuracy in code (97.7\%), whereas DeepSeek leads in math using rubric verification (69.2\%) and in science using chain-of-thought (65.8\%), with models overall favoring direct scoring (Qwen at 73.3\%, Mistral at 67.3\%) or chain-of-thought (DeepSeek at 74.1\%, Llama at 72.3\%). Crucially, evaluating authorship reveals that self-preference is driven entirely by actual model identity rather than prompt framing: models display an overall +11.9\,pp self-preference gap (95\% CI [$+10.4$, $+13.5$]), yet explicitly manipulating the authorship frame shifts accuracy by less than 1\,pp (69.3\% neutral, 69.9\% other, 70.2\% self), demonstrating that prompt anonymization cannot mitigate the bias. This unreliability is fundamentally asymmetric: while verifiers reliably approve correct candidates (88.1\% correct approval rate), they suffer a severe specificity collapse on incorrect candidates, catching only 46.1\% of errors overall and plunging to 26.4\% in math. Our diagnostic probes explain why: style transfer produces negligible shifts in accuracy ($-0.2$\,pp to $-1.5$\,pp), and apparent self-recognition disappears once a model's prior conclusions are held fixed, proving that self-preference is driven by belief persistence---models defending their own mistaken reasoning paths---rather than superficial stylistic fingerprints. Finally, popular mitigations fail to resolve this failure: scaling models and ensembling them yields negligible gains because ungrounded judges share correlated reasoning errors on wrong answers, while ungrounded multi-agent debate juries consistently underperform a single execution-grounded judge despite massive compute inflation. Environmental execution grounding emerges as the sole intervention that reliably restores verifier integrity.

We make four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(i) A controlled factorial protocol and stratified estimator.} We cross belief (self / other / neutral authorship frame) with actual authorship across 4 models, 3 domains, and 3 prompting strategies, holding 779 distinct wrong answers fixed across 6,969 strata for 64,800 total verifications. This design isolates belief effects from confounds that corrupt observational comparisons.
    \item \textbf{(ii) Three mechanism probes refuting the self-recognition hypothesis.} Through a style-transfer probe, a self-recognition probe with consistency control, and a structural self-preference probe, we show that self-preference is not driven by stylistic fingerprints or explicit self-recognition. Once a model's prior conclusion is held fixed, apparent self-recognition collapses from +22.2\,pp to +2.8\,pp ($p = 0.73$), and structural bias is small in absolute terms (3--13\%). This refutes the self-recognition hypothesis of \citet{panickssery2024llm} and demonstrates that self-preference is driven by belief persistence.
    \item \textbf{(iii) Cross-capability oversight and ensemble probes across domains.} We evaluate whether scaling models or ensembling them eliminates evaluator failure. In ungrounded domains (math and science), error detectability does not track capability, and judge panels share correlated errors on wrong answers ($\kappa = 0.35\text{--}0.45$). In the code domain, execution grounding decorrelates errors ($\kappa = 0.09$) and enables an execution-grounded 3-judge panel to catch 89.2\% (majority) to 97.8\% (unanimous) of errors across all generators---though increasing frontier capability paradoxically degrades single-judge oversight ($r = -0.35$) because capable models frequently overrule passing tests. Across the main experiment's code evaluations, our differential fuzzing audit of all 1,458 overrides confirms that 77.2\% (1,125 cases) against passing tests represent hallucinated defects, with only 7.3\% catching genuine bugs. This directly refutes the effectiveness claims of \citet{chan2024chateval} and extends \citet{verga2024replacing}.
    \item \textbf{(iv) Deliberative juries versus execution grounding.} On the hardest code generator (DeepSeek's candidates), we test whether multi-agent deliberation can substitute for execution grounding. Two ungrounded architectures---an unconstrained parallel debate jury and a sequential role-specialized pipeline---catch only 76.7\% and 70.0\% of errors, respectively, while costing $28\times\text{--}450\times$ more than a single judge. On those identical items, a single execution-grounded judge reaches a 93.3\% catch rate (and a grounded panel reaches 96.6\%). We release all 64,800 verification traces, evaluation harnesses, and code.
\end{itemize}
