\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomous agent pipelines rely on large language model verifiers to evaluate candidate outputs and actions \citep{zheng2023judging}. In these systems, the verifier's judgment is load-bearing: it gates tool execution and task completion \citep{shinn2024reflexion}, filters synthetic training data \citep{bai2022constitutional}, and terminates iterative self-refinement loops \citep{madaan2023self}. This architecture rests on a critical assumption: that a model's verdict strictly tracks ground-truth correctness rather than superficial attributes of the text. However, existing literature demonstrates that LLM evaluators are systematically biased. They exhibit well-documented sensitivities to response length and candidate ordering \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large}, alongside a pronounced self-preference where models favor their own generated outputs over those of rivals \citep{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024llm}. When a verifier cannot reliably separate factual validity from its own generative habits, the safety and correctness of the entire autonomous pipeline break down.

To mitigate these evaluator failures, practice has converged on two primary fixes. The first treats self-preference as an authorship conflict of interest: prompts are anonymized to blind the judge to author identity \citep{zheng2023judging,dubois2023alpacafarm}, or models are restricted from grading their own outputs \citep{panickssery2024llm}. The second treats unreliability as variance to be averaged away: pipelines deploy stronger frontier judges, majority-vote panels, or multi-agent debate juries \citep{verga2024replacing,chan2024chateval}. However, it remains an open question whether either strategy actually eliminates the source of these failures rather than merely working around them.

To resolve these questions, we construct a large-scale controlled evaluation spanning 64,800 verifications across four models (Qwen2.5-72B, DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, Mistral-Nemo-12B), three domains (HumanEval+ for code, MATH for mathematics, and GPQA-Diamond for science), and three prompting strategies (direct scoring, chain-of-thought, and rubric-guided). In the main evaluation, verifiers assess each candidate individually with a binary verdict under deterministic decoding ($\text{temperature} = 0.0$), reflecting how verifiers operate in real agent execution loops. We cross believed authorship (framed as self-, other-, or neutrally authored) with actual generator identity, employing an error-stratified estimator that holds 779 distinct wrong answers constant across 6,969 strata to isolate belief effects from candidate difficulty and generator quality. While verification in math and science relies strictly on linguistic reasoning, code verification incorporates runtime execution against unit tests, paired with an automated differential fuzzing audit that validates judge overrides against reference implementations using randomized inputs. Finally, to dissect mechanisms and test proposed mitigations, we deploy five targeted probes: a style-transfer probe, a self-recognition probe with consistency control, a structural self-preference probe, a cross-capability oversight and ensemble probe using leave-one-out non-author panels, and a code deliberation jury probe benchmarking parallel debate and sequential role-specialized architectures on the hardest generator.

Across all 64,800 verifications, models achieve an average accuracy of 69.7\% ($66.9\%$ balanced accuracy), but performance fractures heavily across domains: 87.3\% in code ($85.7\%$ balanced), 64.5\% in math ($60.4\%$ balanced), and 57.2\% in science ($59.2\%$ balanced). Optimal configurations depend strictly on domain and architecture: Mistral achieves 97.3\% in code (direct), whereas DeepSeek leads math (69.2\%, rubric) and science (65.8\%, CoT), with prompt differences in ungrounded domains within noise ($\text{SE} \approx 1.9$\,pp). Crucially, evaluating authorship reveals that self-preference is driven by actual model identity rather than prompt framing, though effect sizes vary across models (severe in frontier models, absent in the 12B model): models display an overall +11.9\,pp self-preference gap (95\% CI [$+10.4$, $+13.5$]), yet manipulating the authorship frame shifts accuracy by less than 1\,pp (69.3\% neutral, 69.8\% other, 70.1\% self), demonstrating that prompt anonymization cannot mitigate the bias. This unreliability is asymmetric: while verifiers reliably approve correct candidates (87.9\% TPR), they suffer severe \emph{specificity collapse}, catching only 46.1\% of errors overall and plunging to 26.4\% in math. Our diagnostic probes explain why: style transfer produces negligible shifts ($-0.2$\,pp to $-1.5$\,pp), and apparent self-recognition collapses once prior conclusions are held fixed, indicating that self-preference is driven by belief persistence---defending mistaken reasoning paths---rather than stylistic fingerprints. Finally, popular mitigations fail: scaling and ensembling yield negligible gains due to correlated errors, while ungrounded debate juries underperform a single grounded judge despite massive compute inflation. Environmental execution grounding emerges as the sole intervention tested that reliably restores verifier integrity.

We make four contributions:
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{(i) A controlled factorial protocol and stratified estimator.} We cross believed authorship (self / other / neutral) with actual generator identity across 4 models, 3 domains, and 3 strategies (64,800 verifications), holding 779 distinct errors fixed across 6,969 strata to cleanly isolate belief effects from true authorship.
    \item \textbf{(ii) Three mechanism probes examining the self-recognition hypothesis.} Using style-transfer, consistency-controlled self-recognition, and structural self-preference probes, we show that in-context self-preference is not driven by stylistic fingerprints or self-recognition. Once prior conclusions are held constant, apparent recognition dissolves, indicating self-preference is driven by \emph{belief persistence}.
    \item \textbf{(iii) Cross-capability oversight and ensemble probes.} We show that in ungrounded domains, error detectability does not track capability, and judges share moderate correlated failures ($\kappa = 0.35\text{--}0.45$), rendering ensemble voting ineffective. In code, our differential fuzzing audit reveals that 77.2\% of judge overrides against passing tests represent unconfirmed defects under automated testing.
    \item \textbf{(iv) Deliberative juries versus execution grounding.} We benchmark ungrounded debate juries against a single execution-grounded verifier on 150 hard code candidates. Deliberation inflates compute costs by $28\times\text{--}450\times$ yet catches only 70.0--76.7\% of errors, while a single execution-grounded judge reaches a 93.3\% catch rate. All evaluation code, harnesses, and traces are available in an anonymized repository.
\end{itemize}
