\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Self-Preference and Recognition.}
Prior work documents that LLM evaluators suffer from systematic position and length biases~\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024large}, as well as pervasive self-bias during self-refinement~\citep{xu2024pride}. Beyond these surface artifacts, \citet{panickssery2024llm} attribute self-preference to active self-recognition, while \citet{wataoka2024selfpreference} link it to familiarity and lower perplexity on self-generated text. In contrast, our evaluation across 6{,}969 error-matched strata indicates that in-context self-preference is not driven by stylistic familiarity or explicit recognition: stripping stylistic markers shifts false approval by at most $-1.5$\,pp, and apparent self-recognition collapses once prior conclusions are controlled for. Instead, self-preference is driven by \emph{belief persistence}—defending erroneous reasoning paths reached during generation.

\paragraph{Self-Verification and Self-Correction Limits.}
A growing body of literature questions whether LLMs can reliably critique and verify reasoning. \citet{huang2024selfcorrect} establish that LLMs cannot autonomously self-correct reasoning without external feedback, while \citet{stechly2024evaluating} and \citet{kamoi2024why} show that unaided self-verification is bottlenecked by severe false-positive rates and confirmation bias. Benchmarks such as JudgeBench~\citep{tan2024judgebench} similarly demonstrate that LLM judges struggle on complex reasoning errors. To address this, tool-interactive frameworks like CRITIC~\citep{gou2024critic} leverage external tools for critiquing. Our work builds on this foundation by providing a controlled factorial decomposition across 64{,}800 verifications: we isolate the cognitive mechanism (belief persistence vs.\ recognition), explain why ensemble voting fails via correlated errors, and benchmark deliberative juries directly against execution grounding.

\paragraph{Evaluation Prompting and Rubrics.}
Prompt design is widely treated as the primary lever for evaluator reliability; notably, \citet{kim2023prometheus} advocate for rubric-guided evaluation. Across our 64{,}800 verifications, we find that the value of rubrics is strictly domain-contingent. In ungrounded reasoning domains, differences between rubrics and chain-of-thought remain within sampling noise ($\sim 1.4$\,pp vs.\ $\text{SE} \approx 1.9$\,pp), failing to resolve specificity collapse. In code with execution grounding, rubric prompting actively degrades accuracy (direct scoring at 92.8\% raw / 96.1\% balanced outperforms rubrics at 83.6\% raw / 83.1\% balanced; Mistral-12B plunges from 97.1\% under direct scoring to 63.6\% under rubrics) by inducing models to overrule passing test suites with hallucinated defects. Rubrics are therefore least helpful—and actively harmful—precisely where ground-truth execution signals exist.

\paragraph{Multi-Agent Deliberation, Panels, and Grounding.}
To resolve single-judge unreliability, \citet{chan2024chateval} propose multi-agent debate, and \citet{rajan2025codexverify} report a 76.1\% code bug catch rate using multi-agent roles. In our code deliberation probe, ungrounded debate catches 76.7\% and sequential role-play catches 70.0\%, while costing $28\times\text{--}457\times$ more compute—still falling far short of a single execution-grounded verifier (93.3\% catch rate). Extending \citet{verga2024replacing}, we reveal \emph{why} ungrounded panels fail: judges share moderate correlated blind spots on wrong answers ($\kappa = 0.35\text{--}0.45$; all judges approve the same error $1.8\times\text{--}2.9\times$ more often than chance), which only decorrelate under execution grounding ($\kappa = 0.09$). The bottleneck in agent verification is the absence of execution grounding, not agent count.
